I flipped a coin ten times and got heads eight times.

**Is the coin rigged, or did I just get a lucky run?**

Statistics has a set procedure for answering that. The number at the centre of
it is the **p-value**.

## You start from the side you are not suspicious of

Statistics does not start at "this coin is rigged!" It starts at the opposite.

- **The null hypothesis** — "the coin is fine." Nothing is going on.
- **The alternative hypothesis** — "the coin is rigged." Something is.

It works like a trial. You **assume** innocence to begin with. Then, if
something happens that the assumption cannot reasonably explain, you drop the
assumption.

## The p-value answers this question

**"If the coin really is fine, how often does something like this happen?"**

If it happens often, there is no reason to suspect the coin.
If it almost never happens, the assumption starts to look wrong.

That much I had. Then I wrote it down and found a missing word.

## The missing words are "or worse"

I had the p-value as **the chance of getting exactly this result**.
It is really **the chance of getting this result or a more lopsided one**.

It sounds like a small difference. It reverses the answer.

| How you count | Chance | Verdict |
|:--|:--:|:--|
| **Exactly 8** heads | 4.4% | Under 5%, so "rigged" |
| **8 or more**, counting the tails side too | 10.9% | Over 5%, so **"cannot call it rigged"** |

Same coin, same data, opposite conclusions.
The only difference is two words.

The reason the worse cases get counted is that the question was never about
one exact number. It is about **how common a run this lopsided is** — so nine
heads and ten heads, being even more lopsided, belong to the same question.

## The second mistake: direction

I had written the conclusion as "so the coin is not fine."

Here is what a p-value actually answers:

> **If the coin is fine**, how likely is a result like this

Here is what we actually want to know:

> **Given a result like this**, how likely is the coin to be fine

**Those are two different numbers.** Swapping the two halves makes it a
different question. A p-value of 1% does not mean "99% chance the coin is
rigged". That is a question the p-value does not answer.

This is why statistics says **"reject"** rather than "false". It is a verdict,
not a proof.

And it runs the same way in reverse. Failing to reject does not prove the coin
is fine. It only means **there was not enough reason to doubt it** — the same
way a not-guilty verdict is not a proof of innocence.

## Who picked 5%?

**Nobody discovered it. A person chose it.**

What matters is that it has to be chosen **before looking at the data**. Pick
the threshold after seeing the result and you can slide it to wherever the
answer you wanted lives. That is not a test, it is an excuse.

And 5% is not free. Test twenty perfectly fine coins and **about one of them
gets wrongly labelled "rigged".** That is the cost that comes with setting the
bar there.

## In short

1. A p-value is the chance of **this result or a more extreme one** — not of
   exactly this result.
2. A p-value is **not the probability that a hypothesis is true.** The
   question runs the other way round.
3. 5% is a threshold people set in advance, and it has a known rate of being
   wrong built into it.
